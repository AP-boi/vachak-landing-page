A voice-first reading program

Every child can learn to read — when someone is listening.

Vachak is an app that listens to a child read aloud in their own language, corrects each mistake in the moment, and turns any adult — parent, teacher, or community volunteer — into a ten-minute daily reading coach. It runs on the phone your family already owns.

The problem

7 in 10 ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries cannot read and understand a simple sentence.

World Bank “learning poverty” estimate, 2022 · updated for post-pandemic learning loss.

  • No one can hear them read.

    Classes of 40 to 60 mean a teacher cannot give each child even one minute of individual reading time a day — and reading only improves when someone hears the errors.

  • The gap closes fast.

    A child who does not decode by the end of grade 3 rarely catches up; almost every later subject depends on reading first.

  • The cost is lifelong.

    Each year of effective schooling raises a child's lifetime earnings by roughly 9% (Psacharopoulos & Patrinos). For the current generation, the losses are measured in trillions of dollars.

How it works

Three things a reading session needs — now automated.

01

It listens

A child reads a short story aloud. Vachak hears exactly where they stumble — the syllable, the word, the long pause — because decoding happens out loud, and out loud is the only place to catch it.

“The cat sat on the mat.” Heard: “ca… ket” — word cat, sound /k/ missing

Who it serves: the child whose teacher has 45 others to hear.

02

It corrects — in the moment

Not “try again.” A model sound, a syllable break, then a re-read of the full line. Correction that lands within two seconds is what builds the decoding pathway; correction that arrives the next day is just a grade.

Vachak: “Break it: c–a–t. Now the whole line again.”

Who it serves: the parent who wants to help but was never taught phonics herself.

03

It levels — to the child, not the class

Every session starts from measured fluency and adjusts. Too-hard text frustrates; too-easy text bores; the growth happens at the edge. Vachak keeps each reader on their own edge, ten minutes a day.

Next session: 14 words in a row read cold — Level 2 → Level 3

Who it serves: the volunteer who can run a reading circle but cannot plan 30 individual lessons.

The adult is never replaced. Parents, teachers, and community workers still run the circle, set the routine, and give the encouragement. Vachak does the part only a 1:1 listener could do before — at a cost of about one to two dollars per child per year.

The evidence

We automate mechanisms that already have proof — not hopes.

A

Oral reading with immediate feedback is among the most reliable interventions in the reading literature — repeated, listened-to reading builds fluency with meta-analytic effect sizes in the d≈0.4–0.7 range (approximate; Therrien 2004 review).

B

Teaching at the right level — Pratham's human-delivered model — regularly moves literacy by 0.3–0.6 SD, but needs trained cadres. Leveling is exactly the part software can automate faithfully.

C

Mother-tongue instruction is the strongest system-level lever in early reading. Vachak is built language-first, not English-first.

  • Sources: World Bank / UNESCO Institute for Statistics, “Learning Poverty” (2022) · Therrien, “Fluency and comprehension gains as a result of repeated reading” (2004) · J-PAL / Pratham, Teaching at the Right Level evaluations.

Trial protocol not results

We measure like we mean it.

Fair questions

The doubts we hear most — answered straight.

If your question is not here, ask us. We would rather lose a signup to an honest answer than win one with a vague promise.

Does this replace teachers?

No — it works through them. The teacher still sets the routine, runs the group, and carries the relationship. Vachak takes over the parts a human cannot do at class size: hearing 45 children read one at a time and tracking each one's level. In the pilot design, sessions are run by existing teachers, parents, and community workers with a short script.

What if the family has no smartphone or no data?

The deployment unit is the phone a family or a village facilitator already owns — no school hardware to buy. The app runs on the cheapest Android devices and works offline between syncs: stories and the voice model download once, sessions run on-device, and progress syncs when data is available. One shared phone can serve a whole reading circle.

Another literacy app — why would this one work?

Most prior apps never listened: they showed text and waited for a tap. The ingredient with the strongest evidence — reading aloud to someone who corrects you immediately — was the one ingredient software could not deliver until speech recognition got cheap and accurate enough for children's voices in low-resource languages. That changed in the last two years. Vachak is built around the listening, not around the screen.

How do you protect children's privacy?

Audio is processed to recognize the reading and then deleted on a short retention policy; no biometric profiles, no advertising, no data resale. Parental consent is required before a child's first session, and every pilot partner signs a data-handling agreement. The full privacy notice ships with the pilot materials.
